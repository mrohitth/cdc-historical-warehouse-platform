## 3. PRODUCT ARCHITECTURE

### Part 1: What Declawing Actually Means

**Chapter 1: The Anatomy of Declawing**
*The reader will be able to:* Understand that declawing (onychectomy) removes the last bone of each toe, not just the keratin claw. This is equivalent to amputating each fingertip at the last knuckle in human terms. Understand why this causes lifelong structural changes to the paw.

**Chapter 2: Phantom Pain and Nerve Damage**
*The reader will be able to:* Understand that amputated limbs can develop phantom pain syndrome, where the brain still sends pain signals to the removed portion. Cats may lick, bite, or shake their paws even years after surgery due to nerve irritation or phantom sensations. This is a medical reality, not a behavioral problem.

**Chapter 3: The Behavioral Ripple Effects**
*The reader will be able to:* Understand why declawed cats are more likely to develop biting habits (they lost their primary defense, so they escalate to teeth), litter box avoidance (associating paw pain with the box), and chronic stress that manifests in various ways.

---

### Part 2: The Pain-Litter Connection

**Chapter 4: Why Litter Hurts**
*The reader will be able to:* Understand that traditional clay litter, silica crystals, and even some natural litters have textures and consistencies that apply pressure or friction to the exposed bone and nerve endings in a declawed paw. This is why cats may wait until they absolutely cannot hold it anymore before using the box — they've learned it hurts.

**Chapter 5: The Association Problem**
*The reader will be able to:* Understand classical conditioning: if using the litter box has repeatedly caused pain, the cat learns to associate the box with pain. This creates avoidance that looks "behavioral" but is actually fear and pain avoidance. Breaking this association requires both pain management AND retraining.

**Chapter 6: Medical Conditions to Rule Out First**
*The reader will be able to:* Understand that declawed cats are at higher risk for UTI, bladder inflammation, and other urinary issues — partly from holding urine longer due to box pain, partly from stress. Know when to get vet tests before assuming it's behavioral.

---

### Part 3: Pain Accommodation Solutions

**Chapter 7: Soft Litter Options That Actually Work**
*The reader will be able to:* Compare litter types for declawed cats — paper-based litters (Yesterday's News, Carefresh), wood pellet litters, cotton litters, and special declaw recovery litters. Know what to try first and how to transition gradually. Key factors: minimal dust (respiratory protection), smooth texture, adequate depth.

**Chapter 8: Litter Box Modifications**
*The reader will be able to:* Understand why standard litter boxes with high walls or liners can be painful — declawed cats may struggle to step over edges or may avoid rough liner textures. Solutions: low-entry boxes, no-liner setup, puppy pee pads as interim solution, box size and placement optimization.

**Chapter 9: Pain Management Options**
*The reader will be able to:* Discuss with their vet: Gabapentin for chronic nerve pain, joint supplements (glucosamine for arthritis in declawed paws), cold laser therapy ( Class IV therapeutic laser shown to reduce phantom and chronic pain), pheromone diffusers for anxiety management. Know what questions to ask the vet about pain management.

**Chapter 10: Soft Paws Nail Caps as an Option**
*The reader will be able to:* Understand that Soft Claws or similar vinyl nail caps can provide a barrier between the paw and litter, reducing pain during box use. Know the application process, duration (4-6 weeks per set), and that they're not a permanent solution but can help during retraining.

---

### Part 4: Retraining and Recovery

**Chapter 11: Breaking the Pain Association**
*The reader will be able to:* Implement confinement retraining: confining cat to a small room with paper bedding or soft fleece on the floor, then gradually introducing a litter box with acceptable litter. The goal: let the cat relearn that the box is not painful, using positive associations (treats, calm environment).

**Chapter 12: When to Consider Rehoming**
*The reader will be able to:* Honestly evaluate whether the cat's quality of life is severely impacted and whether another home environment (one-floor living, outdoor access, dedicated care) might be better. This section validates that sometimes rehoming is the kindest choice — not a failure. Also covers how to rehome a special-needs cat safely.
