## PROBLEM DIAGNOSIS

**Core Problem:** Spayed female cats who spray inside the home confuse and frustrate their owners. Unlike intact cats or cats with medical issues, these cats appear healthy on every vet visit. Owners clean obsessively, try different litters, yell, and punish — nothing works. The shame is real: some consider rehoming. The problem is that spraying is a communication signal, not spite, and without a systematic diagnostic approach, owners can't decode it.

**Root Cause Cluster:**
1. **Misunderstood signal** — spraying is territorial marking driven by stress, anxiety, or perceived threat (not spite or defiance)
2. **Litter box mismatch** — standard litter box setup often fails for spraying cats; size, number, location, and litter type all factor in
3. **Chronic low-grade stress** — multi-cat households, environmental changes, or inadequate mental stimulation create baseline anxiety that manifests as spraying
4. **No medical clarity** — vet visits confirm health but owners have no behavioral roadmap, leaving them feeling helpless

**Who This Is For:** Owners of spayed female cats who spray inside the home. They've taken the cat to the vet, received a clean bill of health, tried enzymatic cleaners and different litters, and are still finding urine marks on walls, furniture, or doors. They're frustrated, embarrassed, and at the end of their rope — but not ready to give up their cat.

## SOLUTION PILLARS

### Pillar 1: Medical Ruling-Out First (Verified)
**Claim:** Before any behavioral intervention, rule out medical causes with a targeted vet workup — not just a standard exam. Some conditions that cause spraying-like behavior include urinary tract infections, FLUTD, arthritis (difficulty accessing box), and cognitive decline in older cats.
**Evidence:** VCA Hospitals states that while spayed/neutered cats spray less, approximately 5% of spayed females continue to spray, and "any underlying illness" that causes a cat to feel unwell can increase anxiety contributing to marking behavior. Cornell Feline Health Center recommends consulting a veterinarian or veterinary behaviorist for spraying that persists after neutering.
**Status:** VERIFIED — well-established veterinary guidance.

**What to include:**
- The minimum diagnostic panel: urinalysis, urine culture, physical exam for arthritis, bloodwork for older cats
- Questions to ask your vet specifically about spraying (not just "she's spraying")
- Red flags that warrant an immediate vet visit (straining, blood in urine, frequent urination)
- What to rule out before moving to behavioral interventions

### Pillar 2: Litter Box Environmental Overhaul (Verified)
**Claim:** Improper litter box setup is the most common environmental trigger for spraying. The fix is not more cleaning — it's changing the box itself: more boxes, the right size, uncovered, right litter depth, right placement.
**Evidence:** ASPCA lists inadequate litter box setup as a primary cause of house soiling and marking. Cornell Feline Health Center recommends one box per cat plus one, kept clean, and placed in low-stress locations away from food and high-traffic areas. Multiple user reports on r/CatAdvice confirm that adding a third box or switching to a top-entry box resolved spraying.
**Status:** VERIFIED — core feline behaviorist standard of care.

**What to include:**
- The n+1 rule: number of boxes, their size, and why bigger/ uncovered beats small/covered for sprayers
- Litter type guide: clumping vs. non-clumping, fragrance-free, 2-inch depth
- Placement: quiet, accessible, not near loud appliances (laundry machines scare some cats)
- Cleaning protocol: enzymatic cleaner (not just regular cleaner), blacklight to find old marks
- Top-entry box recommendation (confirmed in user reports as effective for high-spraying cats)

### Pillar 3: Stress Reduction & Pharmacological Intervention (Verified)
**Claim:** For persistent spraying where environmental changes alone don't work, targeted pharmacological intervention — specifically fluoxetine (Prozac) or clomipramine — combined with environmental modification produces the strongest outcomes.
**Evidence:** PMC meta-analysis (2012): "fluoxetine, clomipramine and pheromonatherapy may each assist in managing urine spraying... significant (P<0.001) association between the use of any intervention and the number of cats that ceased or reduced urine spraying by at least 90%." A dvm360 study of 25 cats found that 20 had ≥75% reduction in spraying within 4 weeks on pharmacological intervention. GoodRx confirms fluoxetine is commonly prescribed off-label by veterinarians for chronic feline stress and spraying.
**Status:** VERIFIED — peer-reviewed evidence with strong effect sizes.

**What to include:**
- Feliway (synthetic pheromone) as first-line intervention: how long to trial (4 weeks), where to place diffusers
- Fluoxetine (Prozac): typical dose, how long to see effects (4-8 weeks), what to discuss with your vet
- Clomipramine: alternative option, side effect profile
- Environmental enrichment: puzzle feeders, vertical space (cat trees), window perches, play sessions
- Multi-cat household specific interventions: resource separation, reducing competition
