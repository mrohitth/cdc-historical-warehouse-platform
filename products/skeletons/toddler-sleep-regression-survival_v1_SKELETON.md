## PROBLEM DIAGNOSIS

**Core Problem:** Parents of toddlers (18mo-4.5yr) who previously had a child who slept well but hit a sleep regression and feel like their child has fundamentally changed. They've tried earlier bedtimes, they've tried letting the toddler cry briefly, they've tried the same bedtime routine they've always used — nothing works. They feel like they've lost their previously good sleeper and don't know how to get them back. The emotional toll is severe: resentment, guilt about yelling, exhaustion affecting their marriage and work.

**Root Cause Cluster:**
1. **Developmental leap disruption** — brain development milestones (language explosion, motor skill gains) cause temporary sleep disruption; the child is practicing new skills during the night
2. **Schedule mismatch** — toddler's sleep needs have dropped but parents haven't shifted the schedule, causing overtiredness that manifests as fighting sleep
3. **Separation anxiety re-emergence** — toddlers cycle through separation anxiety phases; bedtime becomes a battle because the child is genuinely afraid the parent won't come back
4. **Boundary erosion** — previous sleep habits were less solid than parents thought; the regression reveals weak sleep foundations that need rebuilding

**Who This Is For:** Parents of toddlers who previously had a reasonable sleeper and are now in the thick of a sleep regression. They've already tried the basic fixes (earlier bedtime, routine consistency) without success. They're exhausted, slightly desperate, and need to know this is temporary and what to do differently.

**Differentiation from existing catalog:**
- `toddler-sleep-battles` → sleep-onset dependency where the toddler cannot self-transition at bedtime (has never slept well independently)
- This product → previously good sleeper who has regressed. Different problem framing: recovery vs. initial establishment. Different root causes.

## SOLUTION PILLARS

### Pillar 1: Regression Identification + Expected Timeline (Verified)
**Claim:** Not every sleep disruption is a regression. Knowing whether you're in a regression (temporary, 2-6 weeks) vs. a permanent sleep habit change changes your entire response strategy.
**Evidence:** Dr. Marc Weissbluth (Healthy Sleep Habits, Happy Child) documents sleep regressions at 4 months, 8-10 months, 18 months, 2 years, and 2.5 years with typical durations. Parents consistently misidentify the type of problem, leading to ineffective interventions.
**Status:** VERIFIED — supported by pediatric sleep medicine literature.

### Pillar 2: The Regression-Specific Response (Plausible)
**Claim:** Treating a regression like a chronic sleep problem leads parents to overcorrect — implementing full sleep training during a developmental leap when the child genuinely cannot sleep the way they could last week.
**Evidence:** Weissbluth and other pediatric sleep specialists distinguish between "sleep debt" fixes and "developmental" fixes. The appropriate response to a regression is support and consistency, not restructuring.
**Status:** PLAUSIBLE — consistent with clinical guidance but limited controlled studies specifically on regression interventions.

### Pillar 3: Parent Wellbeing During Regression (Plausible)
**Claim:** Parental exhaustion during sleep regressions affects judgment, patience, and the relationship with the child. Brief, targeted self-care and shift-based parenting partnerships significantly reduce the likelihood of reactive parenting.
**Evidence:** Parental sleep deprivation studies (NSF) show measurable impacts on emotional regulation and parenting quality. Shift-based "tag teaming" is widely recommended by parenting coaches.
**Status:** PLAUSIBLE — consistent with general sleep deprivation research; not specifically studied in regression contexts.
