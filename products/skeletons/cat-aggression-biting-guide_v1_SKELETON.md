## PROBLEM DIAGNOSIS

**Core Problem:** Cat owners face sudden, unprovoked biting and attacks from their cats, ranging from "love bites" to serious wounds requiring medical attention. Many owners don't understand why the aggression happens, don't know if it's medical or behavioral, and feel trapped between rehoming and managing ongoing danger—especially concerning for households with children, elderly members, or immunocompromised individuals.

**Root Cause Cluster:**
1. **Misread signals** — Cats give multiple warning signs before biting (tail flick, ear rotation, body tension) that owners miss or misinterpret as affection.
2. **Petting-induced aggression** — Many cats have a threshold for petting; once exceeded, they switch from enjoying touch to defensive biting. Owners don't know the signals.
3. **Play aggression** — Cats who weren't properly socialized for play may attack hands/feet as prey objects. This is especially common in single-cat households or cats adopted during COVID.
4. **Medical pain** — Pain from arthritis, dental issues, or internal conditions can cause defensive aggression. Owners assume behavioral cause when medical may be primary.
5. **Redirected aggression** — A cat agitated by something outside (another cat) may attack the nearest person. Owner has no idea why.

**Who This Is For:** Cat owners dealing with biting or attacking behavior, ranging from occasional "love bites" to serious attacks. Particularly relevant for households with children, elderly owners, or multi-cat homes where aggression causes conflict.

## SOLUTION PILLARS

### Pillar 1: The Aggression Type Diagnostic (Verified)
**Claim:** Identifying the type of aggression is essential because each type requires a different intervention. Misidentifying the cause leads to ineffective or worsening treatment.
**Evidence:** Feline behavior specialists universally emphasize accurate diagnosis before intervention. Cornell University Feline Health Center and ASPCA behavior guidelines both stress that aggression classification guides treatment selection.
**Status:** VERIFIED — aggression typing is standard practice in feline behavior intervention.

**What to include:**
- The 5 aggression types: play, petting-induced, fear, territorial, redirected
- Key distinguishing features for each type
- Red flags requiring immediate vet visit (sudden onset, accompanying lethargy, hiding)
- When to video your cat's aggressive episodes to show the vet
- The vet-first rule: medical causes must be ruled out before behavioral treatment

### Pillar 2: Environmental and Enrichment Modifications (Verified)
**Claim:** Increasing hunting-style enrichment and providing outlets for predatory behavior reduces play aggression and frustration-based biting.
**Evidence:** Feline enrichment research shows that hunting-sequence feeders (puzzle toys, food-dispensing toys) significantly reduce frustration behaviors. Jackson Galaxy's cat behavior program emphasizes "prey sequence" play as foundational for aggression issues.
**Status:** VERIFIED — enrichment is well-documented in feline behavior literature.

**What to include:**
- The prey sequence: hunt → catch → kill → eat (and why it matters)
- Recommended toys by cat type (wand toys, laser pointers, puzzle feeders)
- How to structure play sessions for maximum effect (timing, duration, ending)
- Environmental additions: vertical space, hiding spots, window perches
- When to add a second cat (and when not to)

### Pillar 3: Body Language and Warning Sign Training (Plausible)
**Claim:** Owners who learn to read and respond to feline warning signs can intervene before bites occur, preventing the cycle where bites reinforce owner fear.
**Evidence:** While formal research is limited, feline body language is well-documented in practitioner literature. First-hand accounts from owners who learned to read "whisker tuktuk" or "skin rippling" confirm they could prevent bites once they knew what to look for.
**Status:** PLAUSIBLE — body language signs are documented in practitioner guidance; owner's ability to consistently respond is anecdotal. Present as skill-building, not cure.

**What to include:**
- The pre-bite warning sequence: dilation, ear position, tail movement, body tension
- The "freeze" moment and what it means
- How to safely disengage from an agitated cat
- What NOT to do (punishment, physical forced contact)
- Recovery time: how long after a bite episode to resume normal interaction
