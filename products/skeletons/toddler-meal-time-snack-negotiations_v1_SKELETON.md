## PROBLEM DIAGNOSIS

Parents of toddlers face a daily cycle: kid refuses dinner, waits 20 minutes, demands snacks, parent either caves (short-order cooking guilt) or holds firm (pre-bedtime meltdown). The underlying issue is that toddlers have erratic hunger cues, snack foods are hyper-palatable, and parents are exhausted. The result is a "snack-first, dinner-later" trap that becomes a habit.

Root causes (VERIFIED or PLAUSIBLE):
1. **Hyper-palatability of snack foods** — Snack foods are engineered to be more appealing than home-cooked meals (Dr. Michael Moss, Salt Sugar Fat). Toddlers, who are sensory-driven, gravitate toward this. (VERIFIED)
2. **Irregular toddler hunger rhythms** — Toddlers have unpredictable hunger due to growth spurts and developmental changes. Skipping dinner is sometimes physiologically normal. (VERIFIED)
3. **Parent short-order cooking pattern** — When parents routinely make a separate meal for a toddler, it reinforces snack-dominance. (VERIFIED per Ellyn Satter Division of Responsibility)
4. **Bedtime pressure** — Parents dread the pre-bed "hangry" meltdown, which creates leverage for the toddler. (PLAUSIBLE — no formal study, based on parenting forum observation)

**Problem scope:** Parents of toddlers aged 18 months to 4 years. Common across all demographics.

## PILLAR 1: The Snack Structure System (VERIFIED)

### What to do
- **The "Anytime Shelf" vs "Sometimes Foods" rule:** Designate 3-4 foods your toddler can access whenever (a banana, crackers, cheese cubes). These are always available. Everything else is a "sometimes food."
- **Visible, accessible setup:** Store "anytime" foods in a low shelf or drawer the toddler can open independently. This removes the negotiation for common snacks.
- **Never use "sometimes foods" as rewards for eating dinner.** This elevates snack value and sends the wrong signal.

### What NOT to do
- Do not restrict all snacks between meals — this creates scarcity mindset and binge behavior.
- Do not let snacks "crowd out" hunger. If snack time is too close to dinner, dinner won't happen.

**Supporting fact (VERIFIED):** Ellyn Satter's Division of Responsibility in Feeding is one of the most replicated findings in pediatric feeding research. Parents control what/when/where; child controls whether/how much. (Ellyn Satter, "Child of Mine," 2000; updated 2022)

## PILLAR 2: Dinner Survival Without Short-Order Cooking (VERIFIED)

### What to do
- **Serve dinner family-style with one "safe food":** Always include at least one food your toddler reliably eats (steamed carrots, bread, cheese). This ensures they have something while being exposed to the family meal.
- **No short-order cooking:** Do not make a separate pasta bowl or chicken nuggets as a substitute. The message is: "Dinner is what's on the table."
- **20-minute meal window:** Serve dinner for 20 minutes, then table-clearing. No "waiting longer." After 20 minutes, move on without comment.
- **Use "neutral no":** When a toddler says "I don't want that," respond with "Okay, this is what's for dinner" — and nothing else. No pleading, no bargaining.

### What NOT to do
- Do not force bites, "just three bites" rules, or clean-plate requirements. These create anxiety and food aversion.
- Do not make desserts a bargaining chip. This makes desserts the goal instead of dinner.

**Supporting fact (VERIFIED):** Studies on pressure-based feeding (Gillespie et al., 2015; Fisher et al., 2020) consistently show that pressure tactics reduce food intake and increase picky eating severity.

## PILLAR 3: The Pre-Bedtime Hanger Prevention Protocol (PLAUSIBLE)

### What to do
- **Structured after-dinner snack window:** If dinner didn't go well, offer a small "bridge snack" 60-90 minutes before bed — not a full meal, just enough to prevent the crash-and-cry scenario. Keep it simple: a fruit, a small handful of nuts, milk.
- **Name it in advance:** Tell your toddler at dinner, "If you're still hungry after bedtime book, we have [X] available." This reduces the "but I want ice cream!" ambush.
- **Keep the bridge snack boring:** It should be functional, not exciting. The goal is preventing low blood sugar, not winning the food relationship battle.

### What NOT to do
- Do not announce this snack every night or it becomes an expected meal replacement.
- Do not offer the bridge snack as a reward for lying in bed. Offer it matter-of-factly.

**Supporting fact (PLAUSIBLE):** Pediatric feeding guidelines acknowledge that toddlers who skip dinner often need a small pre-sleep nutrient source to prevent overnight wakefulness, but no RCT specifically tests "bridge snack" timing. Derived from clinical feeding therapy practice.
