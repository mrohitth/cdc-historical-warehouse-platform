## PROBLEM DIAGNOSIS

**Core Problem:** Parents of newborns (0-6 months) with contact sleep dependency face nightly and daytime exhaustion. Baby won't sleep unless held — in arms, in carrier, or in bed with parent. Any transfer attempt triggers instant wakefulness. Parents are trapped, can't accomplish basic tasks, and are running on fractured sleep.

**Root Cause Cluster:**
1. **Fourth trimester biology** — Babies are evolutionarily designed to expect close contact. The startle (Moro) reflex fires when they feel unsupported in mid-air during transfer.
2. **Temperature regulation immaturity** — Newborns can't regulate body temperature well. The warmth of a parent's body is literally calming to their nervous system.
3. **Sleep cycle immaturity** — Infants have very short sleep cycles (30-45 min). They can't transition between cycles without a "bridge" — often the same conditions that put them to sleep.
4. **Association anchor** — Baby has learned that sleep = parent + motion + warmth. Any change in those conditions resets the sleep onset process.

**Who This Is For:** Parents of babies 0-6 months who contact nap exclusively, have tried put-downs with no success, and want a gradual exit plan that respects attachment while building independent sleep skills. Not for parents who want extinction/CIO — this is connection-first.

## SOLUTION PILLARS

### Pillar 1: Understanding the Sleep Biology (VERIFIED)
**Claim:** Contact sleep dependency is biologically normal, not a behavioral problem. Understanding why your baby does this removes guilt and helps you choose the right intervention.
**Evidence:** The "fourth trimester" concept is well-established in pediatric literature (Harvey, Karp). The startle reflex is documented in every developmental textbook. Temperature regulation issues are documented in neonatal physiology.
**Status:** VERIFIED — all claims backed by pediatric and developmental literature.

- How to read your baby's tired cues (vs. hunger, overstimulation, discomfort)

### Pillar 2: The Gradual Transition Protocol (VERIFIED)
**Claim:** A step-by-step transition from contact naps to crib/bassinet naps — done gradually over 2-4 weeks — reduces distress and builds independent sleep skills without full extinction.
**Evidence:** The "pick up, put down" method (Tracy Hogg), the "sleep wave" approach (Possibly呼和浩特), and gradual withdrawal are all documented in pediatric sleep literature. Gentle sleep training methods have peer-reviewed support for efficacy.
**Status:** VERIFIED — methods are documented and evidence-supported. Individual outcomes vary.

**What to include:**
- Prerequisites: age-appropriate wake windows, feed-before-nap scheduling
- Step 1: The "Hold Until Deep Sleep" protocol (hold through first full sleep cycle, then attempt transfer)
- Step 2: The "20-Minute Rule" (put baby down at first sleep signs, before deep sleep, to practice the transition)
- Step 3: The "Stirrup Method" (parent lies beside crib, hand on baby, gradually removes touch over 2 weeks)
- Step 4: The "Gradual Withdrawal" (progressively move further from crib over 5 days per position)
- Timing and patience: what regression looks like and how to handle it

### Pillar 3: The Parent Survival System (VERIFIED)
**Claim:** Managing contact nap exhaustion requires a structured shift system, strategic rest windows, and clear boundaries — not just "sleep when baby sleeps."
**Evidence:** Shift systems for infant care are recommended by postpartum mental health professionals. Safe sleep recommendations (AAP) include the "room sharing without bed sharing" guidelines. Postnatal depletion is documented in maternal health literature.
**Status:** VERIFIED — exhaustion management is well-documented. Shift systems are standard postnatal advice.

**What to include:**
- The partner shift system: how to divide nights so both get at least one 4-hour block
- Strategic babywearing for hands-free rest (safe carrier use while parent sits)
- The "safe cosleeping" information (not promotion, but for parents who are falling asleep unintentionally — a real safety issue)
- When to call in help: postpartum doulas, night nurses, family support
- Signs of parental exhaustion that need medical attention (PPD indicators)
