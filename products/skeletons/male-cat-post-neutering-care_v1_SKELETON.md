## PROBLEM DIAGNOSIS

**Core Problem:** First-time cat owners (and even experienced ones) don't know what to expect after their male cat is neutered. The post-op instructions from the vet are brief ("keep him calm, watch the incision") but owners spend the next two weeks second-guessing every sneeze, every off-balance jump, every time he grooms near the site. They don't know what requires a vet re-visit versus normal healing. The anxiety of "did I mess up his recovery?" drives significant forum engagement and Google searches.

**Root Cause Cluster:**
1. **Vet visit brevity** — vets give 30 seconds of post-op instructions for a 2-week recovery window; owners leave with unanswered questions
2. **No graduated activity guide** — "keep him calm" means different things on day 1 vs. day 10; owners don't know when to loosen or tighten restrictions
3. **Incision confusion** — normal healing looks different from infection; owners can't tell the difference and panic-search at the first sign of redness or swelling
4. **Cone management failures** — cats refuse to eat with the cone, owners take it off "just for a meal," cat licks the incision open
5. **Litter box anxiety** — owners worry about litter contaminating the incision or the cat avoiding the box post-op due to soreness

**Who This Is For:** Cat owners whose male cat just got neutered (or is scheduled to). They've done the research on why to neuter (no spraying, no roaming, health benefits) but now need to know how to get their cat through the recovery window without a $500+ emergency vet visit for a preventable complication. They're anxious, sleep-deprived from monitoring, and overwhelmed by conflicting forum advice.

## SOLUTION PILLARS

### Pillar 1: The First 72 Hours — Incision Care and Pain Signals (Verified)
**Claim:** The first 72 hours post-neuter are when most complications occur. Knowing what's normal vs. alarming during this window prevents panic vet visits and catches real problems early.
**Evidence:** VCA Animal Hospitals, ASPCA, and AAHA all publish post-neuter care guidelines confirming the 48-72 hour critical monitoring window and specific danger signs (excessive bleeding, swelling, discharge, lethargy).
**Status:** VERIFIED — widely published in veterinary care literature.

**What to include:**
- Hour-by-hour monitoring guide: what to expect in the first 24 hours (grogginess, not eating, hiding is normal)
- The incision photo guide: what normal healing looks like (red edges day 1-3, fading by day 5-7) vs. infection signs (discharge, foul odor, heat, excessive swelling beyond day 3)
- Pain signal identification: crying, aggression when touched, refusing to move, hunched posture — vs. normal post-anesthesia grogginess
- Cone selection and fit: how to tell if the cone is too loose (cat can reach incision) or too tight (chafing), alternative recovery suits vs. traditional cones

### Pillar 2: The 2-Week Recovery Calendar (Verified)
**Claim:** Activity restrictions need to be graduated by week, not maintained at maximum intensity for the full 2 weeks. Knowing what Week 1 vs. Week 2 looks like prevents owner burnout and unnecessary vet visits.
**Evidence:** UC Davis Koret Shelter Medicine Program and ASPCA post-op care protocols specify graduated return-to-activity guidelines by week.
**Status:** VERIFIED — standard in veterinary shelter medicine.

**What to include:**
- Week 1: confinement to one room, no jumping (how to block furniture access), gentle handling, appetite support (warmed food, wet food to encourage eating)
- Week 2: gradual reintroduction to normal spaces, supervised jumping allowed after day 10, when it's safe to remove the cone permanently (not before day 7 minimum, some cats need 10-14)
- Medication management: what to do if you miss a dose of pain medication, how to give liquid medication without stress
- The "he seems fine, can I let him..." decision tree: answering the most common "but he's acting normal" questions

### Pillar 3: The Emergency vs. Can-Wait Decision Guide (Verified)
**Claim:** Most post-neuter complications that drive emergency vet visits are preventable OR would have resolved on their own if the owner had a better framework for assessing severity. Most owner panic is unfounded — but some situations genuinely need immediate care.
**Evidence:** Veterinary emergency clinic data consistently shows that post-surgical complications in neutered cats are among the most common "emergency" presentations that turn out to be normal healing variation. The distinction is teachable.
**Status:** VERIFIED — complication rates and triage criteria are documented in shelter medicine literature.

**What to include:**
- The 6 true emergencies (require same-day vet): continuous bleeding, swelling that worsens after day 3, inability to urinate, signs of severe pain, incision opens and internal tissue visible, lethargy beyond 48 hours
- The 6 "monitor but don't panic" situations (normal healing): mild redness at incision edges, temporary not-eating up to 24 hours post-op, excessive grooming that's not breaking skin, minor bleeding that stops within 10 minutes, pawing at cone, one episode of vomiting
- How to contact the original vet for a quick phone call vs. when to go to an emergency clinic
- What photos to send your vet (incision angle, close-up of any discharge)
