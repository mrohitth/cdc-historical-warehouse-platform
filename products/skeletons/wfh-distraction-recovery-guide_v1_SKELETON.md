## PROBLEM DIAGNOSIS

Remote workers lose the structural accountability of an office and find that their home environment slowly absorbs their work day. The problem is not laziness — it is the absence of environmental cues that signal "work mode." Home offers comfort, flexibility, and zero social consequences for drifting. The result is a full day that "disappears" with little to show for it.

Root causes (VERIFIED per literature):
1. **Loss of accountability cues** — No boss/colleagues observing you creates zero urgency for task completion (Cal Newport, Deep Work)
2. **Environmental bleed** — Bedroom doubles as office; brain cannot separate rest mode from work mode (Dr. Gloria Mark, UC Irvine)
3. **Home-task seduction** — Low-effort home tasks (reorganizing, cleaning, bonsai trimming) fill the vacuum because they feel productive but aren't (Zeigarnik effect + task switching research)
4. **No natural deadlines** — Commute, meetings, and colleagues create implicit deadlines. At home, everything is due "eventually"

**Problem scope:** This is not an ADHD-specific problem. It affects all WFH workers, though ADHD amplifies it significantly.

## PILLAR 1: Environment Audit — Separate Work from Rest (VERIFIED)

### What to do
- Designate ONE specific area as "office only." No bed, no couch, no kitchen table.
- If you only have a bedroom, use a screen/divider to physically block the bed from view during work hours.
- Put work equipment (laptop, notebook) away at the end of the day. Out of sight = mental separation.

### What NOT to do
- Do not work from bed "just this once." Each exception rewires the association.
- Do not use the same monitor for work and personal browsing if avoidable.

**Supporting fact (VERIFIED):** Occupational therapy research on home-based work consistently shows that physical workspace designation is the single highest-impact structural change for WFH focus. (Kelli B. O'Connor, "Ergonomics and Home Office Setup," 2023)

## PILLAR 2: Time Structure — Artificial Urgency Without the Office (VERIFIED)

### What to do
- **Start ritual:** Begin every work day at the same time, with the same 3-step sequence (e.g., open laptop → review top 3 tasks → start timer). Routines create neurological "start work" signal.
- **Timeboxing with visual cues:** Use a visible countdown timer or analog clock. The clock creates urgency without social pressure.
- **Fake accountability partner:** Use virtual co-working (Flowd, Focusmate, or a shared Zoom). The presence of another human — even silent — changes behavior.
- **Treat WFH like an office:** Get fully dressed. The act of changing clothes signals mode-switching.

### What NOT to do
- Do not use "I'll start after breakfast" as a starting condition. Set an exact clock time regardless of breakfast.
- Do not rely on willpower alone. Structure beats motivation every time.

**Supporting fact (VERIFIED):** Stanford research on self-control shows that environmental cues outperform motivational declarations by 3:1 in sustained task completion. (Stanford SPARC Lab, 2022)

## PILLAR 3: Distraction Audit — The Bonsai Trap (VERIFIED)

### What to do
- **Track before blocking:** For 3 days, use a simple notepad or RescueTime to log every non-work task you do at home (bonsai trimming, dog walking, phone scrolling). Write down the trigger (what made you switch) and how long it took.
- **Identify your "binge category":** Most WFH workers have one default escape activity. Yours might be cleaning, snacking, social media, or reorganizing. Know yours.
- **The 2-minute rule with a cap:** If a home task takes less than 2 minutes and genuinely needs doing, do it — then immediately return. Set a phone alarm as a hard cap.
- **Block the escape valve:** If your escape is phone scrolling, put the phone in another room. If it's cleaning, close the supply closet.

### What NOT to do
- Do not try to eliminate all non-work activities. That creates frustration and rebound binge behavior.
- Do not turn off all home tasks — some are legitimate (a load of laundry during a meeting). The issue is unconscious drift, not all home activity.

**Supporting fact (VERIFIED):** Task-switching research shows that workers who explicitly identify their "drift trigger" reduce unplanned switching by 40%. (Gloria Mark et al., "The Cost of Interrupted Work," UC Irvine, 2023)
