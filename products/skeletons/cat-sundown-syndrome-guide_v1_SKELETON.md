## PROBLEM DIAGNOSIS

**Core Problem:** When a senior cat suddenly begins yowling at night, owners assume it's behavioral and try attention-retraction techniques that fail — because the real cause is cognitive decline, sensory loss, or sundown syndrome. Owners feel guilty for being frustrated, vet visits yield "nothing wrong," and they're left with no real plan. The cat is confused and frightened; the owner is desperate for sleep and peace.

**Root Cause Cluster:**
1. **Feline Cognitive Dysfunction (CDS)** — a form of dementia affecting 50%+ of cats 11-15 years old (Cornell Feline Health Center). Disorientation at night is a hallmark sign.
2. **Hearing loss** — deaf cats yowl louder because they can't self-monitor volume. Onset can be sudden after illness or gradual with age.
3. **Arthritis and pain** — senior cats with joint pain may cry out when settling, especially in the middle of the night when the house is cold and surfaces feel harder.
4. **Anxiety from sensory decline** — a cat losing vision or hearing at night becomes disoriented in a dark, quiet house. The howling is a distress call.
5. **Hyperthyroidism** — increases vocalization, especially at night, and is common in cats 10+. Often missed in early stages.

**Why standard advice fails:** Behavioral guides assume an attention-seeking cat. CDS cats aren't seeking attention — they're frightened and lost. Ignoring them worsens the distress. The owner needs a medical-first, comfort-focused protocol, not behavior modification.

**Who This Is For:** Owners of cats 10+ who have begun yowling at night in the past 6-12 months. They've tried ignoring it, white noise, calming plugins. They feel guilty for being frustrated. They want to help their cat, not just stop the noise.

## SOLUTION PILLARS

### Pillar 1: Medical Triage — Rule Out the Treatable (Verified)
**Claim:** Before any behavioral intervention, senior night yowling requires veterinary screening for hyperthyroidism, arthritis, hypertension, and pain. These are all treatable and may eliminate or reduce the yowling.
**Evidence:** Cornell Feline Health Center, AVMA: hyperthyroidism is the most common endocrine disease in cats over 10, causing increased vocalization. Arthritis affects 60-90% of cats over 12 (Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery). Hypertension causes disorientation and crying.
**Status:** VERIFIED — firmly established in feline veterinary medicine.

**What to include:**
- Senior cat vet visit checklist: specifically request T4 panel, senior chemistry, blood pressure, arthritis palpation
- Hyperthyroidism: the "golden triad" — weight loss + increased appetite + behavioral change (including night yowling)
- Arthritis signs that vets often miss: reluctance to jump up/down, slower on stairs, sleeping in different spots
- Pain as a vocalization driver: especially common in cats with dental disease or CKD
- Hypertension: often secondary to kidney disease; causes head tilt, disorientation, crying

### Pillar 2: CDS and Sensory Loss Environmental Support (Verified)
**Claim:** Cats with CDS or sensory decline need a specifically designed nighttime environment: night lights throughout the home, consistent furniture placement, pheromone support, and comfortable rest stations on every floor.
**Evidence:** Cornell Feline Health Center confirms altered sleep-wake cycles and disorientation are hallmark CDS signs. AAHA 2025 article on compassionate CDS care recommends consistent environment, night lights, and pheromone therapy. AAFP guidelines support environmental modification as first-line CDS support.
**Status:** VERIFIED — established in feline geriatric care literature.

**What to include:**
- Night light mapping: one night light per dark hallway, near litter boxes, at landing points between rooms
- Litter box accessibility audit: no steps required, at least one box per floor, low-entry boxes for arthritic cats
- Consistent furniture placement: never move the cat's bed or favorite resting spots
- Feeder placement: keep food and water on the same floor where the cat sleeps
- Pheromone strategy: Feliway Classic for general anxiety, Feliway Optimum for CDS-related anxiety
- Nighttime routine consistency: feed, play briefly, and settle at the same time every night
- Audio enrichment: soft music or nature sounds at a consistent volume can calm disoriented cats

### Pillar 3: Owner Emotional Support and Quality-of-Life Planning (Verified)
**Claim:** Caring for a cat with CDS is emotionally devastating. Owners need permission, practical coping strategies, and honest guidance on recognizing decline milestones and end-of-life decisions.
**Evidence:** The human-animal bond research (AVMA, AAHA) consistently shows caregiver burden in chronic illness pet owners. AAHA's 2025 quality-of-life framework specifically addresses end-of-life decision-making for pets with cognitive decline.
**Status:** VERIFIED — recognized in veterinary hospice and palliative care literature.

**What to include:**
- The "I'm not a bad owner" reframe: this is a disease process, not a behavior problem
- Sleep deprivation survival guide for the caregiver: earplugs, white noise, rotating sleeping arrangements
- When to consider temporary medication (gabapentin, amitriptyline): what's appropriate, what requires a vet prescription
- Quality-of-life scale: a practical rubric for tracking decline
- Euthanasia timing: honest guidance on recognizing when the bad nights outweigh the good days
- Practical preparation: pre-planning end-of-life care reduces crisis decisions
- Memorial and grief support: resources for after loss
