## PROBLEM DIAGNOSIS

**Core Problem:** New cat owners are financially blindsided by vet costs. The median unexpected vet bill for a cat owner is $1,000-$3,000. Critical emergencies (urinary blockage, poisoning, trauma) routinely run $3,000-$12,000. Most new owners have zero financial plan for this. The result is impossible triage decisions at the worst possible moment.

**Root Cause Cluster:**
1. **"Cats are cheap" myth** — The cultural narrative says cats are low-cost pets compared to dogs. Reality is that vet costs are similar, just less predictable.
2. **No insurance literacy** — Most new owners don't understand how pet insurance actually works (reimbursement model, pre-existing conditions, coverage caps).
3. **No emergency fund habit** — Humans without pets rarely have a pet emergency fund. First major bill = crisis.
4. **Confusion about what counts as an emergency** — Owners either over-react to minor issues (driving up costs) or under-react to genuine emergencies (delaying care).

**Who This Is For:** New cat owners (first year) who want to plan ahead and avoid financial crisis. Also useful for prospective adopters who want to understand true cost before committing.

## SOLUTION PILLARS

### Pillar 1: The Real Cost of Cat Ownership (Verified)
**Claim:** Most first-time cat owners underestimate annual costs by 40-60%. The gap is driven by vet care and emergencies, not food or supplies.
**Evidence:** ASPCA annual survey, pet insurance claim data, multiple first-hand owner accounts of "I had no idea."
**Status:** VERIFIED — cost data is well-documented.

**What to include:**
- Itemized first-year cost breakdown: adoption through 12-month vet visit
- Monthly recurring costs (food, litter, preventive meds)
- Emergency cost range for the 10 most common cat emergencies
- Cost comparison: indoor-only vs. outdoor cat annual costs

### Pillar 2: Pet Insurance vs. Self-Insure (Plausible)
**Claim:** The insurance vs. self-insure decision depends on income stability, cat breed health profile, and cash reserves — and there's a mathematically defensible answer for each situation.
**Evidence:** Pet insurance industry data shows ~80% of claims are under $500; the argument for self-insuring is stronger for predictable, smaller costs; insurance wins for true emergencies.
**Status:** PLAUSIBLE — the math is sound but depends heavily on individual circumstances. Guide will present the framework, not force a specific answer.

**What to include:**
- How pet insurance actually works (reimbursement, not cashless in most cases)
- What policies cover, exclude, and cap
- Breed-specific hereditary risk guide (Persian, Maine Coon, Siamese health profiles)
- The self-insure math: how much to save monthly to reach $5k in 12 months
- Decision tree: which approach fits which situation

### Pillar 3: Building a Pet Emergency Fund (Verified)
**Claim:** A dedicated pet emergency fund of $1,500-$3,000 covers ~90% of common cat emergencies. It takes 6-12 months to build through small monthly allocations.
**Evidence:** Most common cat emergencies (UTI, dental disease, wound care, ingested foreign body) fall in the $500-$2,500 range. Major emergencies (blockage, trauma) require $3,000-$8,000 but are less common.
**Status:** VERIFIED — cost data from vet clinics and emergency animal hospitals.

**What to include:**
- Step-by-step: how to build a $2,000 pet fund in 6 months ($333/month)
- Savings account vs. separate fund vs. emergency credit line (pros/cons)
- The "three tiers" framework: what you can handle yourself, what requires the fund, what requires insurance
- What to do in a real emergency (step-by-step crisis protocol)
