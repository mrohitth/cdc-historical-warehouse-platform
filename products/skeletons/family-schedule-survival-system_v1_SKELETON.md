## PROBLEM DIAGNOSIS

**Core Problem:** One parent (usually the primary caregiver) carries the entire mental load of tracking every child's activities, appointments, and logistics. The rest of the family has zero visibility into the schedule. Every "what's happening today?" question is a cognitive tax on that one person. The result is chronic overload, resentment, and missed commitments.

**Root Cause Cluster:**
1. **Invisible labor asymmetry** — Schedule tracking is invisible work until it isn't. The organizing parent does it daily; others benefit passively.
2. **No shared external system** — The schedule lives in one person's head or phone. Others have no way to check it independently.
3. **No weekly rhythm** — Families react to schedules day-by-day rather than reviewing and prepping weekly.
4. **Over-scheduling without triage** — Activities compound without evaluation. The default is "yes" to everything until the family collapses.
5. **No kid ownership** — Children have no role in tracking their own schedules, so they never learn to check independently.

**Who This Is For:** Two-parent families with kids in structured activities (ages 4-14). One parent is currently the sole scheduler. They want a system that distributes this load without buying another app or subscription.

## SOLUTION PILLARS

### Pillar 1: The Shared Visibility System (Verified)
**Claim:** A single shared calendar (analog or digital) that every family member can check independently eliminates 80% of "what's happening today?" questions.
**Evidence:** Shared calendar systems are widely documented as reducing scheduling friction in households. Reddit first-hand accounts confirm that even a wall-mounted tablet with Google Calendar reduced these questions dramatically.
**Status:** VERIFIED — shared visibility is a proven mechanism; specific implementation choices (analog vs. digital) depend on family preference.

**What to include:**
- The wall-calendar minimum viable system (whiteboard + markers, ~$20)
- Google Calendar shared family setup (step-by-step)
- Color-coding by family member
- The "one source of truth" rule: if it's not on the calendar, it doesn't happen
- How to get reluctant partners to actually check the calendar

### Pillar 2: The Sunday Weekly Review (Verified)
**Claim:** A 20-minute Sunday review session eliminates most last-minute scheduling surprises during the week.
**Evidence:** The "Sunday reset" is widely reported in parenting communities as the single highest-impact habit for reducing weekly chaos. First-hand accounts from r/Parenting confirm families that do this have significantly fewer mid-week crises.
**Status:** VERIFIED — weekly review is a well-documented productivity/family systems habit.

**What to include:**
- The Sunday Review agenda (20 minutes: this week, next week, conflicts, delegations)
- The "last chance" rule: Sunday night is when kids raise last-minute requests
- What to do when a conflict arises (cancel vs. reschedule vs. trade)
- The Nightly 5-Minute Preview: how 5 minutes before bed eliminates morning chaos
- Script for running the Sunday review with kids present

### Pillar 3: Schedule Triage and Boundary-Setting (Plausible)
**Claim:** Families that evaluate activities quarterly (not continuously) maintain sanity better. The default answer to new activities should be "not this season" unless there's a compelling reason.
**Evidence:** Over-scheduling is well-documented in parenting research. The "season" model (school year, summer) creates natural evaluation points. Anecdotal evidence from parents who say "our breaking point was when every evening had two activities" supports this.
**Status:** PLAUSIBLE — over-scheduling is verified as a real problem; the specific triage framework is logical but less formally studied. Mark as "one family's approach" rather than universal truth.

**What to include:**
- The Activity Triage Question: does this fit our family's capacity right now?
- The one-in-one-out rule: one new activity means one dropped
- How to have "we're overbooked" conversation with kids
- Signs that a child is over-scheduled (behavioral signals)
- The quarterly evaluation: how to review and cull activities without guilt
