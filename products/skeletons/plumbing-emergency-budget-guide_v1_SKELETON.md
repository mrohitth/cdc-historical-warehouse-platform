## 3. PRODUCT ARCHITECTURE

### PART 1: THE PRICING LANDSCAPE

*What you're actually walking into*

**Chapter 1: The Emergency Surcharge Explained**

The reader will: Understand why emergency plumbing calls cost 2-3x more and what components make up the total bill.

Key facts:
- Standard plumber hourly rate: $85-$150/hr (2025 data)
- Emergency/after-hours premium: additional $50-$150 per visit, or 1.5-2x hourly rate
- Weekend premium: many plumbers add $25-$75 flat fee for Saturday calls
- Holiday premium: up to 2x standard rate on major holidays
- "Emergency" vs. "urgent" vs. "standard" — definitions vary by company, ask upfront
- Trip/service charge: $50-$150 just to show up, often applied to the repair bill

Verification: VERIFIED — multiple plumbing company websites and consumer guides confirm pricing tiers.

**Chapter 2: The Common Repairs Cost Guide**

The reader will: Reference realistic cost ranges for the 15 most common plumbing problems that drive emergency calls.

Cost reference table (verified ranges):
- Leaky faucet: $150-$350
- Running toilet: $100-$300 (flapper replacement is DIY-eligible for $5 part)
- Clogged drain (clear): $150-$400
- Water heater repair: $200-$800
- Water heater replacement: $800-$3,000
- Leaky pipe repair: $150-$350 (accessible); $500-$2,000+ (hidden)
- Garbage disposal repair/replace: $150-$400
- Sewer line clog: $200-$1,000
- Slab leak: $500-$4,000+
- Sump pump replacement: $250-$750
- Well pump repair: $250-$900
- Shut-off valve replacement: $150-$400
- P-trap replacement: $100-$250
- Supply line replacement: $50-$150
- Toilet replacement: $200-$800

Verification: VERIFIED — sourced from HomeAdvisor, Angi, Plumbing Pricing Data (2025-2026).

**Chapter 3: The Time Factor**

The reader will: Understand how long common repairs take and how billing structure affects total cost.

Key facts:
- Minimum service call duration: 1 hour billed even if job takes 15 minutes
- Flat-rate vs. hourly billing — flat rate is often more expensive for simple jobs but predictable for complex ones
- Diagnostic fee: $75-$175 — sometimes waived if you proceed with repair
- After-hours minimum: many companies have a 2-hour minimum for emergency calls

### PART 2: THE DEFENSE PLAYBOOK

*How not to get overcharged*

**Chapter 4: The Pre-Call Checklist**

The reader will: Gather information before calling a plumber that will help them get accurate quotes and avoid upsells.

Pre-call checklist:
1. Identify the exact problem (faucet dripping, pipe leaking, toilet running, etc.)
2. Locate the shut-off valve for the affected fixture — know if you can shut it off
3. Take photos of the problem area
4. Note when the problem started and any changes
5. Know your home's plumbing layout (first floor, second floor, basement)
6. Have your home's age and any recent plumbing work noted

**Chapter 5: The 5 Questions to Ask Before Hiring**

The reader will: Screen plumbers over the phone to avoid predatory operators and get accurate pricing.

5 questions:
1. "What is your service call fee and does it apply to the repair bill?"
2. "Do you charge emergency/after-hours rates? What's the multiplier?"
3. "Is this a flat-rate quote or an estimate subject to change?"
4. "Are you licensed and insured? Can you give me your license number?"
5. "Can you give me a ballpark range for this specific repair over the phone?"

Red flag responses: refusing to give ballpark estimates, insisting on showing up before discussing price, cash-only, high-pressure upselling.

Verification: VERIFIED — standard consumer advice from Better Business Bureau and state contractor associations.

**Chapter 6: Getting 3 Quotes Without Getting Stuck**

The reader will: Compare multiple quotes effectively and recognize when a low quote is a bait-and-switch.

Key facts:
- Angi, HomeAdvisor, Thumbtack for quote comparison
- Get at least 3 quotes for repairs over $300
- "Bait and switch" red flags: very low ballpark quote that explodes on-site
- What to ask when quotes vary wildly: "Can you itemize the quote? What's included?"
- Never sign blank or verbal contracts

### PART 3: THE COMMON TRAPS

*Where homeowners lose money*

**Chapter 7: The "While We're Here" Trap**

The reader will: Recognize and resist the upsell that happens when a plumber suggests additional work "while they're already on-site."

Common upsells:
- "Your shut-off valve looks old, should replace it while we're here" — maybe needed, maybe not; get a separate quote
- "This pipe is corroded, might as well replace it" — not urgent unless actively leaking
- "We'll need to replace the other shut-off for balance" — unnecessary unless there's an actual problem
- "Your water heater is 8 years old, better to replace it now" — depends on condition, not age alone

Strategy: "That's interesting, I'd like to get a separate quote for that. Can you put today's repair in writing first?"

**Chapter 8: The After-Hours Trap**

The reader will: Make intelligent decisions about whether they actually have an emergency or can wait for standard business hours.

Decision framework:
- Active spray of water (pressure leak): emergency, call now
- Slow drip under sink with bucket underneath: can wait until morning
- Toilet overflow: emergency if on second floor (risk of ceiling damage below); can wait if on first floor with towels
- Sewage backup: emergency, call now
- No water in entire house: emergency, call now
- Running toilet: can wait

Key message: "If water is not actively spraying and there is no risk of structural damage, you can almost always wait until morning and save 2x the rate."

**Chapter 9: The Permit Trap**

The reader will: Understand when permits are actually required and when a plumber uses them to inflate the bill.

Key facts:
- Permit requirements vary by municipality and repair type
- Small repairs (faucet, toilet, P-trap) almost never require permits
- Water heater replacement often requires permit ($50-$200)
- Re-piping, sewer work, slab leak repair may require permits
- Ask: "Is a permit required for this repair? If so, who obtains it and is that included in your quote?"

### PART 4: PREVENTION AND PREPAREDNESS

*Stop emergencies before they start*

**Chapter 10: The Annual Plumbing Checkup**

The reader will: Conduct a basic annual plumbing inspection that catches problems before they become emergencies.

Annual checkup checklist:
1. Check all visible supply lines for corrosion, rust, moisture
2. Test shut-off valves — turn on and off, make sure they work (valves that haven't been used in years often seize)
3. Check water heater age and anode rod
4. Flush water heater (sediment buildup causes premature failure)
5. Check water pressure (40-80 psi is normal; above 80 risks pipe damage)
6. Inspect under all sinks annually for early leak signs
7. Check toilet stability (rocking toilets often have slow leaks at the wax ring)
8. Test sump pump

**Chapter 11: Building Your Plumbing Emergency Fund**

The reader will: Calculate the right emergency fund target for their home and understand what to have on hand vs. what to call for.

Key facts:
- Standard recommendation: $500-$1,000 for minor plumbing emergencies
- $2,000-$5,000 for homes with older plumbing (pre-1990)
- $5,000+ for homes with known risk factors (slab foundation, cast iron pipes, water heater over 10 years)
- Home warranty insurance: $300-$600/year, covers some but not all plumbing — read the fine print for coverage caps and exclusions
