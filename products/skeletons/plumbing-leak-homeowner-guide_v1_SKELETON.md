## 3. PRODUCT ARCHITECTURE

### PART 1: THE ASSESSMENT

*Learn to identify what you're actually dealing with*

**Chapter 1: Read the Leak — A Classification System**

The reader will: Classify their leak into one of five categories (slow seep, active drip, pressure leak, hidden leak, slab leak) using a visual decision tree. They will understand the urgency tier for each category and what timeframe they're working with.

Key facts to include:
- Slow seep: Seeping water at joints or connections. Often from a worn washer or loose fitting. Can be DIY if accessible.
- Active drip: Continuous dripping from a visible point. Often a damaged cartridge or corroded pipe. DIY possible if localized; otherwise pro.
- Pressure leak: Water spraying or streaming under pressure. Shut off water immediately. Call pro — do not attempt DIY.
- Hidden leak: Water inside walls, under floors, or in ceiling cavities. Signs: musty smell, discoloration, bubbling paint, soft drywall, warped baseboards, unexplained high water bill. Requires professional leak detection ($175-$500, up to $1,000+ for complex).
- Slab leak: Leak in pipes under concrete foundation. Signs: hot spot on floor, sound of running water under slab, cracks in foundation. Requires professional rerouting or jackhammering ($500-$3,000+).

Verification: ACTIVE — multiple plumbing sources confirm classification and urgency tiers.

**Chapter 2: The Shut-Off Protocol**

The reader will: Locate and operate their main water shut-off valve and individual fixture shut-off valves. They will understand when shutting off water is the correct first response (pressure leaks, before any repair attempt).

Key facts to include:
- Main shut-off typically near water meter or where main line enters house
- Individual shut-offs under sinks and behind toilets
- Gate valve vs. ball valve — ball valves are quarter-turn and more reliable
- If you hear water running when nothing is on, shut off immediately — you have a pressure leak somewhere

Verification: ACTIVE — standard plumbing knowledge, multiple sources confirm.

**Chapter 3: The Leak Severity Calculator**

The reader will: Use a 7-point assessment rubric to determine whether their specific leak warrants immediate professional call, scheduled professional call, or DIY repair. They will calculate approximate cost exposure for each path.

Assessment rubric points:
1. Location: exposed vs. hidden
2. Flow rate: drip vs. stream
3. Duration: how long it has been present
4. Progression: is it getting worse?
5. Access: can you reach the pipe easily?
6. Pressure: is it under main pressure?
7. Evidence of damage: stains, mold, softness

Output: Red (call pro now), Yellow (schedule pro within 72hrs), Green (DIY viable)

Verification: VERIFIED — derived from standard plumbing inspection protocols.

### PART 2: DIY OR DON'T

*The decision framework*

**Chapter 4: What You Can Actually Fix**

The reader will: Identify which plumbing issues fall within DIY range for a non-handy homeowner. They will understand the specific tools and materials required for basic leak repairs.

DIY-viable situations:
- Worn washer replacement in faucet (requires parts, $5-$15)
- Supply line replacement (braided stainless steel, $10-$20)
- P-trap leak at joint (threaded connection, plumber's tape)
- Toilet flapper replacement ($5 flapper, 10-minute job)
- Showerhead replacement (no torch needed)

NOT DIY:
- Anything requiring soldering or torch work
- Pressure leaks on main lines
- Hidden leaks inside walls
- Anything involving gas lines
- Slab leaks

Verification: ACTIVE — confirmed by multiple DIY plumbing guides.

**Chapter 5: The Cost Estimation Tool**

The reader will: Generate a realistic cost estimate for their repair before calling a plumber. They will understand the difference between flat-rate and hourly billing and what questions to ask on the phone.

Key facts:
- Plumber hourly rates: $85-$150 (2025 data)
- Small exposed leak repair: $150-$350
- Leak detection service: $175-$500 (complex/hidden: up to $1,000)
- Slab leak repair: $500-$3,000+
- Questions to ask: "Is this a flat rate or hourly?" "What's included in the service call?"
- Get 3 quotes via Angi, HomeAdvisor, or Nextdoor

Verification: VERIFIED — sourced from Plumbing Tips Today, A to Z Statewide Plumbing, Redwood Plumbing (2025-2026 data).

**Chapter 6: Finding a Reliable Plumber**

The reader will: Identify trustworthy plumbing services and avoid common scams. They will know what documentation to request and understand warning signs of bad actors.

Key facts:
- Licensure check: ask for license number and verify via state contractor board
- Insurance: confirm liability insurance before work begins
- Estimates: get written estimates, not verbal
- Deposit rules: no more than 10-20% upfront, and only for materials
- Red flags: cash-only, refuses to provide documentation, high-pressure sales tactics

Verification: VERIFIED — standard consumer advice for hiring contractors.

### PART 3: THE ACTION SEQUENCE

*What to do based on what you found*

**Chapter 7: If You Can DIY — The Fix Sequence**

The reader will: Execute a safe, basic plumbing repair using a step-by-step sequence. They will understand when to stop and escalate.

For each DIY-viable fix:
- Shut off water at local valve
- Drain the line (open lowest faucet)
- Apply repair (washer, tape, replacement part)
- Test before restoring full flow
- Check for leaks at connection points

Emphasis: If at any point you're uncertain, stop. Call a pro. "Amateur confidence with plumbing can be more dangerous than the problem itself."

**Chapter 8: If You Need a Pro — The Call Sequence**

The reader will: Make an effective service call that gets accurate help fast. They will know what information to have ready and what to expect.

Information to prepare:
- Leak classification and location
- Photos of the leak and surrounding area
- Water bill history (any spike?)
- What you've already tried
- Access situation (crawlspace? basement?)

What to expect:
- Service call fee ($85-$150 typical)
- Diagnosis + estimate on first visit
- Repair quote vs. replacement recommendation
- Timeline for work

**Chapter 9: Protecting Your Home While You Wait**

The reader will: Implement temporary containment measures to prevent further damage while arranging repair. They will understand what is safe to do temporarily and what is not.

Temporary measures:
- Buckets and towels for active drips
- Tarps for ceiling leaks
- Do NOT use tape or epoxy as a permanent fix — temporary only
- Do NOT ignore a slow leak waiting to "see if it gets worse"
- Document everything with photos for insurance purposes

Key message: "Any leak left untreated for more than 48 hours has the potential to develop mold, damage structural members, or void your homeowner's insurance coverage for the event."

### PART 4: PREVENTION AND MAINTENANCE

*Keep this from happening again*

**Chapter 10: The Annual Plumbing Audit**

The reader will: Conduct a basic annual inspection of their home's plumbing system to catch problems before they become emergencies. They will know the 8-point checklist for evaluating their plumbing health.

Annual audit checklist:
1. Water meter check (any movement with all water off = leak somewhere)
2. Supply line inspection under every sink
3. Water heater status (age, anode rod, sediment)
4. Main shut-off operation (exercise the valve)
5. Toilet tank components (flapper, fill valve)
6. Outdoor spigots and irrigation lines
7. Water pressure check (40-80 psi is normal range)
8. Visible pipe condition (corrosion, rust, mineral buildup)

**Chapter 11: What Breaks First**

The reader will: Understand which plumbing components have the shortest lifespans and plan accordingly. They will know what proactive replacements can prevent emergency calls.

Component lifespans:
- Supply lines (braided): 10-15 years
- Faucet cartridges: 8-12 years
- Water heater tank: 8-12 years
- Toilet flapper: 5-7 years
- Gate valves: 10-20 years (but can seize if not operated)

Proactive replacement signals: age + any visible corrosion = budget for replacement now rather than emergency replacement later.
