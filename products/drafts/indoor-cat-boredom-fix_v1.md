## Page 3: The Enrichment Hierarchy

Not all enrichment is equal. If you have limited time and budget, spend it on the highest-impact activities first.

**Tier 1: Food-Based Enrichment (Most Impact)**

Cats are hunters. The hunting sequence is: stalk, chase, pounce, catch, kill, eat. Domestic cats get the last step delivered in a bowl. The first five steps are missing.

Food puzzles restore the hunt. A cat that works for its food uses more energy, stays mentally sharper, and is significantly less likely to engage in destructive attention-seeking behavior.

The research is clear on this. Certified cat behaviorist Stephanie Merlin states that "things like knocking items off tables, scratching furniture, chewing on objects, or persistently seeking attention can all be a cat's way of saying, 'I'm bored and I need more to do.'"

Food puzzles address this directly. A cat that is mentally engaged hunting kibble is a cat that is not tearing apart your couch.

Start here. Everything else is secondary.

**Tier 2: Vertical Space**

Cats feel safer in elevated positions. A cat that has access to high perches is a calmer cat. Cat shelving, window perches, and cat trees give cats territory that feels secure.

Vertical space also doubles the usable square footage of your home from your cat's perspective. More territory means less boredom.

**Tier 3: Interactive Play**

Daily interactive play sessions target the hunting instinct directly. The "hunting game" before meals is the most effective single intervention for most destructive behaviors. Fifteen minutes of active play with a wand toy, followed by feeding, mimics the natural hunt-eat-sleep cycle.

**Tier 4: Environmental Complexity**

Rotate toys, move furniture slightly, introduce new textures. Cats orient to changes in their environment. A static environment is a boring environment.

Work through these tiers in order. If you are short on time, do Tier 1. That alone will move the needle.

## Page 4: Food Puzzles - Which Type For Which Cat

Not all food puzzles work for all cats. Here is the breakdown:

**Puzzle Feeders (treat-dispensing)**

Best for: Cats new to enrichment. Start here if your cat is skeptical.
Examples: Catit Senses 2.0, Trixie Pet Games, Outward Hound Nina Ottosson
Price range: $10-$25

These require the cat to bat the toy to release kibble. Low frustration, quick success.

**Maze Feeders**

Best for: Medium-experience cats. Provides more challenge.
Examples: Doc Popnplay's Popbox, Cat Amazing
Price range: $20-$40

These require the cat to move parts to access food. Good for cats that finish puzzle feeders too fast.

**DIY Enrichment**

No budget? Use a cardboard egg carton with holes punched in it. Stuff with kibble and close it. The cat works to open it. Cost: free.

Toilet paper tubes with both ends folded in, holes punched, kibble inside. Same principle.

**How to Introduce Food Puzzles**

Day 1-3: Place the puzzle near the regular bowl. Do not require the cat to use it. Let them investigate.

Day 4-7: Put a small amount of kibble in the puzzle. Leave the regular bowl available. If they eat from the puzzle, success. If not, try a different puzzle style.

Week 2: Reduce the amount of kibble in the bowl. Increase the amount in the puzzle.

Week 3+: Puzzle is the primary food delivery method. Bowl is empty or minimal.

## Page 5: The 30-Day Enrichment Plan

This is a structured introduction. Do not skip ahead. Cats adjust better to gradual changes.

**Week 1: Food Puzzle Foundation**

Days 1-3: Introduce one puzzle feeder. Place it next to the food bowl. Let the cat investigate without pressure.
Days 4-7: Start using the puzzle for 25% of meals. Monitor how the cat responds.
Weekend: If the cat is using the puzzle consistently, move to Week 2. If not, try a different puzzle style before proceeding.

**Week 2: Vertical Space**

Install one cat shelf or window perch at a height the cat already uses. You are adding territory, not creating an obstacle course.
Cats that have access to elevated resting spots show measurably lower stress markers. Pick a spot near a window. Viewing birds is bonus enrichment.

If you cannot install shelving, a cat tree in a corner works. Look for solid base stability. Cats will not use a wobbly tree.

**Week 3: Interactive Play Protocol**

The 15-minute hunting game before the evening meal.
Use a wand toy with a feather or fabric attachment. Move it erratically, not fast. The goal is to mimic prey movement: short dashes, stops, direction changes.
After 15 minutes of play, feed the cat. The play session followed by food mimics the hunt-eat sequence.
Most owners report a noticeable behavior change within 2 weeks of consistent play sessions.

**Week 4: Environmental Rotation**

Start rotating which toys are available. Put half the toys in a box for a week, then swap.
Slightly rearrange furniture. Move a chair two feet. Add a cardboard box with a blanket.
These small changes provide novelty without overwhelming the cat.

**After Day 30**

By this point, food puzzles are normal, the cat has vertical territory, you have a daily play habit, and environmental rotation is ongoing. The behaviors you saw in Week 1 should be significantly reduced.

## Page 6: Interactive Play - The Hunting Game Protocol

Interactive play is not optional. It is the most direct way to address the underlying hunting instinct that drives destructive behavior.

**The Setup**

Wand toy with a prey-like attachment. Do not use your hands as the toy. This teaches the cat that hands are prey.
A dragging string on the floor is not the same thing. Erratic, prey-like movement is the requirement.

**The Session**

Start when the cat is somewhat alert, not when they are in deep sleep. The pre-meal window works best.
Move the toy in a figure-8 or random pattern across the floor. Stop occasionally.
When the cat makes contact, let them "catch" the toy briefly before resuming.
End with the cat catching the toy and you releasing it. Then feed.

**How Long**

15 minutes. Not 2 minutes. Not 30 minutes. 15 minutes is the research-backed sweet spot for engaging the hunting sequence without over-stimulating.

**Common Mistakes**

Moving the toy too fast. Real prey does not move at high speed constantly.
Stopping too soon. The cat needs to complete the sequence: stalk, chase, catch, kill.
Playing at the wrong time. Before meals is when hunting behavior peaks in cats.

## Page 7: Vertical Space Solutions

**Cat Shelving**

Cat shelving is installed wall-mounted at different heights. It creates a climbing path across a room.
Best for: Small apartments where floor space is limited.
Cost: $20-$60 per shelf for basic models. Professional installation adds cost.
Placement: Start by observing where your cat already climbs or sits at elevation. Mount shelving that connects existing high points.

**Window Perches**

The simplest vertical addition. Clamps onto the window sill or suction-mounts to the window glass.
Best for: Renters who cannot install shelving. No tools required.
Look for: Perches with a padded surface and solid suction cups. Test the weight rating before trusting it.

**Cat Trees**

Freestanding climbing structures. Best for: Multiple cats, homes with limited wall space.
Look for: Wide base for stability. Multiple platforms at different heights. sisal-wrapped posts for scratching.
Avoid: Trees that wobble when the cat climbs. An unstable tree will not be used.

**Budget Alternatives**

Stack two bookshelf cubes (IKEA Kallax works) to create a climbing structure. Secure to the wall for safety.
Use the top of a bookshelf as a perch. Clear a space, add a blanket.

## Page 8: When Boredom Is Actually a Medical Problem

Boredom and medical issues look similar. Here is how to tell the difference before you assume it is an enrichment problem.

**Rule Out First: See Your Vet If You See These**

Sudden litter box avoidance. This is a medical signal until proven otherwise. UTI, FLUTD, kidney disease, diabetes. All cause cats to eliminate outside the box. Do not treat this as a behavioral problem until a vet has ruled out medical causes.

Excessive grooming that causes bald patches. This can indicate pain, allergies, or neurological issues. Enrichment will not fix this.

Aggression that appears suddenly, especially if directed at the same household member. Pain causes irritability. A cat that suddenly bites when touched in a specific spot may be protecting a painful area.

Changes in appetite, water consumption, or weight. Any of these warrant a vet visit.

**Boredom vs. Enrichment Deficit**

A bored cat is active and engaged in destructive behavior. A cat with a medical issue often becomes withdrawn and inactive.

Bored cats show increased energy when enrichment is introduced. Cats with underlying medical issues often do not.

When in doubt, start with a vet check. Then add enrichment. Do not substitute enrichment for veterinary care when medical signs are present.

## Page 9: Quick-Reference Checklist

Use this checklist to assess and improve your cat's enrichment:

- [ ] Food puzzle introduced and in regular use (Tier 1)
- [ ] Vertical space available: cat shelf, perch, or tree
- [ ] 15-minute interactive play session happens daily before evening meal
- [ ] Toys are rotated weekly (half in storage, half available)
- [ ] Environmental changes made in past month (rearranged furniture, new items)
- [ ] Sudden behavioral changes checked with vet first (Rule Out Medical)
- [ ] Litter box issues discussed with vet before behavioral intervention
- [ ] 30-day enrichment plan completed and behaviors assessed
- [ ] Budget allocated for ongoing enrichment supplies ($10-20/month)
- [ ] Baseline behavior documented before enrichment changes
- [ ] Progress noted weekly for first 8 weeks
