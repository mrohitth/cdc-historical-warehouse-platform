# Stop Fixed Female Cat Spraying: The Diagnostic Guide

**Your spayed cat is trying to tell you something. Here's how to listen.**

---

For cat owners whose spayed female won't stop spraying. You've been to the vet. She's healthy. You've cleaned until your hands cracked. She's still going. This guide is for you.

---

## Introduction: Why Your Cat Is Spraying (And Why It's Not About You)

Your cat is spraying. You've probably already tried:
- Different litters
- More frequent scooping
- Enzymatic cleaners
- Yelling
- Squirt bottles
- Putting her outside more

None of it worked. Maybe it got worse.

Here's the thing: spraying is not spite. It's not punishment. It's not your cat being "bad."

Spraying is a signal. Cats mark territory when they feel unsafe, stressed, or anxious. It's their way of saying: *I need more resources, or I feel threatened, or something in this environment is wrong.*

When a spayed female sprays despite being healthy, the problem is almost always behavioral or environmental — not medical. And because the vet says "she's fine," owners leave with no roadmap and feel helpless.

This guide gives you that roadmap.

---

## Page 1: The Spraying vs. House Soiling Distinction

Before you do anything else, confirm you're dealing with spraying — not ordinary house soiling. The solutions are different.

**Spraying markers:**
- Small amounts of urine on vertical surfaces (walls, doors, furniture sides)
- Cat stands with tail-up, vibrates tail, sprays backward
- Usually a strong-smelling, concentrated urine (even if she eats the same food)
- Often near windows, doors, or in rooms with other cats

**House soiling markers:**
- Large puddles on horizontal surfaces (floors, rugs, beds)
- Cat squats like normal elimination
- Often near the litter box (avoidance behavior)
- May be constipation or urination (different medical causes)

If your cat is squatting and peeing puddles on the floor, this guide still applies, but start with Page 3 (Medical Ruling-Out) first — puddles are more likely to have a medical cause.

If she's spraying on walls and doors, you're in the right place.

---

## Page 2: The Five Things Every Spraying Cat Owner Needs to Hear

**1. It's not your fault.** Cats don't spray to hurt you. They spray because something in their world is wrong.

**2. Punishment doesn't work.** Yelling, squirt bottles, and discipline make spraying worse. Your cat isn't making a choice — she's responding to stress. Punishment raises her stress level, which increases spraying.

**3. You've already done the right thing by going to the vet.** Medical causes need to be ruled out first. That's not a dead end — it's a starting point.

**4. There's a specific order to solving this.** Ruling out medical causes comes first. Then litter box setup. Then stress reduction. You can't skip steps — doing them out of order wastes time.

**5. This takes weeks, not days.** Any intervention — pheromones, medication, litter box changes — needs a minimum 4-week trial before you judge whether it worked. Set a calendar reminder.

## Page 3: Medical Ruling-Out: What to Ask Your Vet

Your vet said she's healthy. That's a good start. But "healthy" in a general exam isn't the same as "ruled out for spraying-related conditions."

**The minimum diagnostic panel for a spraying cat:**

| Test | Why It Matters |
|------|----------------|
| Urinalysis | Rules out urinary tract infection (UTI), crystals, bladder stones |
| Urine culture | Detects hidden bacterial infection that urinalysis can miss |
| Physical exam including joint palpation | Arthritis in older cats can make it painful to access the litter box |
| Bloodwork (if cat is 8+) | Rules out hyperthyroidism, kidney disease, diabetes |

**Questions to ask your vet:**
- "Have we ruled out a urinary tract infection with a culture?"
- "Is there any sign of arthritis that might make box access uncomfortable?"
- "Could this be idiopathic (stress-related) spraying, or should we investigate further?"

**Red flags that mean emergency vet visit:**
- Straining to urinate with little or no urine production
- Crying or vocalizing during urination
- Blood in urine
- Lethargy combined with any urinary symptoms

If all tests come back clean, you've confirmed what you suspected: this is behavioral. Now you can stop guessing and start working the problem systematically.

## Page 4: Why Stress Manifests as Spraying

Spraying is a stress-response behavior. When cats feel anxious — even at low levels that don't show as obvious distress — they mark their territory to create a more predictable, controlled environment.

**Common stress triggers for spayed females:**
- New cat in the neighborhood (even outside your home)
- New person in the household
- Moving furniture or rearranging rooms
- Construction noise outside
- Multi-cat household tension
- Inadequate resources (not enough perches, hiding spots, or attention)
- Dirty litter boxes (from the cat's perspective, not yours)

**The feline stress scale:**
Cats experience stress on a continuum. Small stressors accumulate. Your cat might not be "freaking out" — she might just be sitting in a slightly elevated stress state all the time, and that chronic low-level anxiety drives the spraying.

Reducing even one or two stressors can make a measurable difference.

## Page 5: The Litter Box Overhaul

If medical causes are ruled out, your first and most important intervention is the litter box setup. This is not about cleaning more — it's about changing the boxes themselves.

**The n+1 rule:**
The standard recommendation is one litter box per cat, plus one. If you have two cats and one is spraying, you need three boxes minimum. Place them in different locations — not side by side.

**Size and type matter for sprayers:**
- Bigger is better. A large plastic storage bin (like Sterilite) with an entry hole cut in the lid gives more room than a standard litter pan.
- Top-entry boxes work well for sprayers who back up to spray on walls — they can't aim upward as easily.
- Avoid covered boxes if your cat is the only cat using them. Covered boxes trap odor inside and can make cats feel trapped.
- If you have multiple cats, covered boxes can cause one cat to ambush another at the entrance.

**Litter:**
- Clumping, unscented litter is generally preferred by cats. Scented litters can deter some cats.
- Depth: about 2 inches. More is not better.
- If you've switched litters recently, consider switching back — some cats dislike the texture of crystal or wheat-based litters.

**Placement:**
- Quiet locations away from loud appliances (washing machines, dryers can scare some cats)
- Not next to food and water
- Easy access at all times — don't close doors that block box access
- If you have multiple floors, at least one box per floor

**Cleaning protocol:**
- Scoop daily. Yes, daily.
- Full dump and scrub weekly with unscented soap (no bleach, which can be aversive to cats).
- Enzymatic cleaner on all sprayed spots — regular cleaner isn't enough to break down the proteins that signal "spray here again" to your cat.
- Blacklight (UV flashlight, ~$15 on Amazon) to find old spray marks you can't see.

## Page 6: Multi-Cat Household Challenges

If you have more than one cat, spraying is more common. More cats = more potential for resource competition and perceived threats.

**Signs of multi-cat tension:**
- One cat follows the other from room to room
- Staring contests at feeding time
- One cat always eats alone or in an unusual location
- Hissing or swatting at the litter box
- One cat hiding more than before

**What to do:**
- Add more resources. Each cat should have their own food bowl, water station, scratching post, and hiding perch. Vertical space (shelving, tall cat trees) lets cats use different levels without contact.
- Feed cats in separate rooms if there's competition at mealtimes.
- Add another litter box in a different area — not just near the existing one.
- Consider feliway multi-cat or feliway friends (synthetic calming pheromones) in the areas where tension is highest.
- If one cat is being bullied, create a "refuge" room with a box, food, and perches that the other cat cannot access.

**Temporary separation:**
If the spraying cat and another cat are fighting or if the spraying started after a new cat arrived, a gradual reintroduction may be needed. Keep the cats fully separated (different rooms, no visual contact) for 1-2 weeks, then introduce smells (swap bedding), then visual contact through a gate, then supervised time together.

## Page 7: Pheromones, Medication & Enrichment

**Feliway (synthetic pheromone) — first-line behavioral intervention:**

Feliway is a synthetic copy of the feline facial pheromone — the one cats deposit when they rub their face on objects to mark them as safe. The idea is that a Feliway-scented environment feels more "claimed" and safe, reducing the need to spray.

- Use Feliway Classic diffusers in the rooms where spraying happens most. One diffuser covers about 700 square feet.
- Trial length: minimum 4 weeks. You won't see full effects before then.
- FeliwaySpray can be applied directly to sprayed spots (after cleaning) to reduce repeat marking.
- Feliway Multicat (different formula) is for multi-cat household tension specifically.

**Fluoxetine (Prozac) — pharmacological intervention:**

When environmental changes and pheromones aren't enough, medication can help. Fluoxetine is the most studied and most commonly prescribed medication for feline spraying.

- Off-label use in cats (not FDA-approved specifically for spraying, but widely used and accepted in veterinary behavior medicine).
- Typical starting dose: 0.5–1 mg per kg of body weight, once daily.
- Effects take 4–8 weeks to become apparent. Don't judge it at 2 weeks.
- Common side effects: mild sedation, reduced appetite (usually temporary).
- Needs to be prescribed by your vet. Some cats stay on it long-term; others taper off after the spraying stops.

**Clomipramine — alternative medication:**

An alternative to fluoxetine, sometimes used when fluoxetine isn't effective. Has a more sedating effect. Same veterinary prescription required.

**Important:** Medication is not a substitute for environmental changes. The drugs work best when combined with litter box improvements, enrichment, and stress reduction.

**Environmental enrichment — the missing piece many owners skip:**

Cats need more than a litter box and food. Mental stimulation reduces baseline stress.

- **Play sessions:** 10-15 minutes of active play (wand toys, laser pointer) per day. Mimics hunting. A tired cat is a calmer cat.
- **Puzzle feeders:** Makes meals take longer and provides mental engagement.
- **Vertical space:** Cat trees, shelving, window perches. Cats feel safer up high.
- **Hiding spots:** Cardboard boxes with entry holes, covered beds. Cats need bolt-holes.
- **Rotating toys:** A few toys out at a time, swapped weekly so they stay interesting.

## Page 8: The 30-Day Intervention Tracker

Don't try to do everything on Day 1. Work the steps in order and track your results.

**Week 1:**
- [ ] Schedule vet appointment for spraying workup (urinalysis, culture, physical exam)
- [ ] Order enzymatic cleaner if you don't have it
- [ ] Buy a blacklight and check for old spray spots
- [ ] Add one more litter box if you have fewer than n+1

**Week 2:**
- [ ] Vet appointment completed — review results
- [ ] If medical causes ruled out: implement litter box changes (size, type, placement)
- [ ] Plug in Feliway diffusers in key rooms
- [ ] Start 10-minute daily play sessions

**Week 3:**
- [ ] Scrub all identified spray spots with enzymatic cleaner
- [ ] Assess multi-cat tension and add resources as needed
- [ ] Research fluoxetine with your vet (ask at the appointment)
- [ ] Add puzzle feeders or enrichment toys

**Week 4:**
- [ ] Evaluate: is spraying frequency changing?
- [ ] Note which interventions you've been consistent with
- [ ] If no improvement, schedule follow-up to discuss fluoxetine
- [ ] Continue all environmental changes regardless — they're working even if you can't see it yet

**Important:** You need a full 4-week trial of each intervention before judging effectiveness. One bad day or one new spray spot doesn't mean it's not working. Track total incidents per week.

## Page 9: Quick-Reference Checklist

**Vet Visit Prep:**
- [ ] Request urinalysis + culture (not just dipstick)
- [ ] Ask about arthritis if cat is 7+
- [ ] Ask specifically about spraying — describe frequency, location, and when it started
- [ ] Get bloodwork quote for cats 8+

**Litter Box Setup:**
- [ ] Number of boxes = number of cats + 1
- [ ] At least one box per floor
- [ ] Big, open-top bins (not small standard pans)
- [ ] Clumping, unscented litter, 2-inch depth
- [ ] Scooped daily; full dump weekly
- [ ] Boxes in quiet, accessible locations

**Stress Reduction:**
- [ ] Feliway diffusers running in problem rooms
- [ ] Daily play sessions (10-15 min)
- [ ] Puzzle feeders at mealtimes
- [ ] Vertical space and hiding spots available
- [ ] Multi-cat resources separated if needed

**Medication (vet discussion):**
- [ ] Fluoxetine discussed as option if 4+ weeks of environmental changes didn't work
- [ ] Dosage confirmed with vet
- [ ] 4-8 week trial commitment set

**Cleaning:**
- [ ] Enzymatic cleaner restocked
- [ ] Blacklight used to find all spray spots
- [ ] All marked spots scrubbed and re-checked weekly

## Page 10: Next Steps

### What's Working for Other Cat Owners

The combination approach — vet ruling + litter box overhaul + Feliway + fluoxetine for persistent cases — is the most evidence-backed path to stopping spraying. The PMC meta-analysis found that interventions reduced spraying by 90% or more in most cats treated.

If your cat has been spraying for months, this won't fix in three days. But it will fix.
