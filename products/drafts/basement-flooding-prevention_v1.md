## PAGE 3: WHERE IS THE WATER COMING FROM?

Before you can fix the problem, you need to know what kind of water problem you have. They are not the same.

**Type 1: Surface Water Intrusion**
Water enters through foundation cracks, window wells, or gaps around pipes. This typically happens during heavy rain when soil becomes saturated. You will see water on walls or pooling near the edges of the basement.

**Type 2: Groundwater Seepage**
The water table rises and water pushes through the floor or cove joint (where floor meets wall). This is a slow, persistent problem in some geographies and requires exterior drainage solutions.

**Type 3: Sewer Backup**
Water backs up through floor drains when the municipal sewer system overloads. This is the most damaging type — it involves contaminated water and often happens the fastest, with the least warning.

This guide focuses on Type 3: sewer backup through floor drains during heavy rain.

### How to Tell Which Type You Have

Answer these questions:

1. Does water only appear during or immediately after heavy rain? If yes → likely surface water or sewer backup.
2. Does water appear even during dry weather? If yes → likely groundwater seepage.
3. Does water come up through the floor drain specifically? If yes → sewer backup.
4. Does the water smell unusual (like sewage)? If yes → sewer backup.

If you answered yes to #3 and #4, you have a sewer backup problem. Keep reading.

## PAGE 4: THE FLOOR DRAIN FAILURE MODE

Here is what happens:

Your home's drain system is connected to the municipal sewer. Under normal conditions, water flows from your home into the sewer, and gravity does the work.

During heavy rain, the municipal sewer system gets overwhelmed. Stormwater and wastewater share the same pipes in combined sewer systems (common in older cities). When the system fills up, pressure reverses. Water that should flow out instead pushes back toward your home.

The floor drain is the lowest opening in your basement. It is also the first point of entry for backed-up water.

Why standard drain covers do not work:

A drain cover or drain plug stops water from going down the drain. It does not stop water from being pushed up through the drain by pressure. When the municipal system is pushing water back toward your home at 10 PSI, a rubber drain cover is not going to hold.

The only effective solution is a backwater valve — a mechanical device that allows water to flow out but closes when pressure reverses.

---

## PAGE 5: THE PRIMARY DEFENSE — BACKWATER VALVE

A backwater valve is a one-way valve installed in your drain line where it exits your home. When water tries to flow backward, the valve closes. When pressure equalizes, the valve opens again automatically.

There are two main types:

**Check Valve**
A spring-loaded valve that closes automatically when pressure reverses. Simple, reliable, low-maintenance. Best for homes with straightforward drain line configurations.

**Gate Valve**
A manually operated valve that you close before a storm and open after. Requires human action — which means you need to know a storm is coming. Not ideal if you travel or if storms come on suddenly.

For most homeowners, a check valve is the better choice.

### What It Costs

- Materials: $100-$300 for the valve itself
- Installation: $200-$600 depending on whether excavation is required
- Total typical range: $300-$800
- Some municipalities offer subsidies: up to $1,250 in some Canadian cities

### What Installation Involves

The valve must be installed where your drain line exits the foundation — either outside (requiring excavation) or inside (through a cleanout access point). Interior installation is less disruptive and less expensive. Not all configurations allow interior installation; a plumber can determine which approach works for your home.

Installation typically takes one day. Permits are required in most jurisdictions.

## PAGE 6: THE WATER REMOVAL ENGINE — SUMP PUMP

A backwater valve prevents sewage from entering. A sump pump removes water that accumulates around your foundation — whether from groundwater seepage, minor seepage, or any water that does enter despite your precautions.

Here is how it works:

1. A basin is installed in the lowest point of your basement floor.
2. Water collects in the basin.
3. When water reaches a certain level, the pump activates.
4. The pump pushes water through a discharge line, away from your foundation.

### Submersible vs. Pedestal

**Submersible pumps** sit inside the basin, submerged in water. They are quieter and more efficient. They cost more upfront ($200-$400) but last longer.

**Pedestal pumps** sit above the basin with a pipe extending into the water. Less expensive ($100-$200) but noisier and less powerful.

For most homes, a submersible pump is the better choice.

### What It Costs

- Pump unit: $150-$400
- Basin and installation: $350-$800
- Total typical range: $500-$1,200

## PAGE 7: THE NON-NEGOTIABLE — BATTERY BACKUP

The worst time for a sump pump to fail is during a storm. Storms knock out power. No power means no sump pump. A flooded basement during a power outage is exactly the scenario you are trying to prevent.

A battery backup is not optional if you want real protection.

### Options

**Battery Backup Pump**
A secondary pump that activates when the primary pump fails or the power goes out. Runs on a deep-cycle marine battery (12V). Can move 2,000-3,000 gallons per hour. Runtime: 4-8 hours depending on battery capacity and water volume.

Cost: $200-$400 for the backup pump and battery.

**Water-Powered Backup**
If your home has municipal water pressure (not well water), a water-powered backup pump can run indefinitely as long as water is flowing. Uses the Venturi effect to move water. Does not require electricity.

Cost: $300-$600 installed.

**Generator Integration**
A portable generator can power your sump pump and other essentials during an outage. Requires manual setup and fuel. Better for extended outages but requires more effort.

For most homeowners, a battery backup pump is the right choice. It installs quickly, costs less than a generator, and activates automatically when power fails.

## PAGE 8: EARLY WARNING — WATER ALARMS

Water alarms are not prevention. They are detection. But detection matters.

A $30 water sensor placed near your water heater, washer, furnace, or floor drain will alert you immediately when water is present. If you are home during a storm and hear the alarm go off, you can take action — move valuables, call a plumber, set up fans.

Smart water sensors go further. They send a push notification to your phone when water is detected, even if you are not home. If your basement starts flooding at 2am while you are asleep or while you are at work, you will know.

### What to Buy

- Basic water alarm: $15-$25 each. Loud sound when triggered. Good for basements with a drain.
- Smart water sensor: $40-$80 each. Sends phone alerts. Some models also monitor temperature and humidity.

Place sensors near:
- Water heater
- Washer
- Furnace or boiler
- Floor drain
- Any other water source in the basement

This is the $50 step you can take this weekend. It does not prevent flooding, but it gives you time to respond.

## PAGE 9: YOUR PROTECTION ROADMAP

Not sure where to start? Here is a tiered approach based on your budget.

### Tier 1: Essential ($50-$200)

Start here regardless of budget.

- Install water alarms near every water source in the basement
- Test your existing sump pump monthly (pour a bucket of water, verify it activates)
- Clear debris from floor drains and gutter downspouts
- Elevate valuables and storage items off the basement floor
- Make sure your downspouts direct water at least 6 feet from your foundation

### Tier 2: Standard Protection ($500-$1,500)

For homes with an existing sump pump that lacks battery backup:

- Install a battery backup pump system: $200-$400
- For homes without a sump pump: Install a complete sump pump system: $500-$1,200

### Tier 3: Comprehensive Protection ($1,500-$3,000)

For maximum protection:

- Backwater valve installation: $300-$800
- New sump pump with battery backup: $700-$1,500
- Smart water sensor network: $150-$300

### What Order to Do Things In

If you are on a budget and need to prioritize:

1. First: Battery backup for existing sump pump (if you have one). Power outages during storms are the most common failure mode.
2. Second: Backwater valve. Prevents sewage from entering.
3. Third: New sump pump (if you do not have one). Handles groundwater and any seepage.
4. Fourth: Smart water sensors. Early warning system.
