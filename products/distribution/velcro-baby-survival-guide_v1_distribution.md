Body:

I want to talk about something that doesn't get enough airtime despite affecting huge numbers of families: the Velcro baby phase, specifically the "non-preferred parent" dynamic.

This is the parent whose baby lights up the moment the other parent walks in the room — and who screams or goes cold and indifferent when they pick the baby up.

I know that pain. Hearing someone say "she just wants me" when your own 5-month-old looks at you with blank indifference is genuinely devastating. It can trigger real depression and resentment. It puts enormous strain on partnerships.

Here's what I learned, from looking into the actual developmental neuroscience:

**This is a neurological milestone, not a verdict on your bond.**

Around 6-10 months, infants develop object permanence. They understand that people exist even when they can't see them. This sounds like a cognitive advance, and it is — but it comes with a cost. Your baby now experiences your leaving the room as an actual crisis. When they see the preferred parent — the one whose face most reliably produces comfort — they attach hard. When they see you, they don't have the same accumulated trust map.

This is not your baby rejecting you personally. It is stranger danger and separation anxiety running exactly as designed.

**What actually helps:**

The non-preferred parent needs consistent, low-pressure exposure — not marathon sessions that end in tears. A daily 20-minute ritual of fully present, device-free engagement does more than sporadic long visits. Skin-to-skin contact with the non-preferred parent has direct physiological effects on infant calm. If you're bottle feeding, having the non-preferred parent take over one feed per day is one of the highest-leverage interventions available.

**The preferred parent needs permission to step away.** Being the person your baby can't live without sounds flattering until you haven't had five minutes to yourself in three months. Touch saturation is real. Guilt about stepping away is common. Neither helps anyone.

**The couples piece matters.** The Velcro dynamic accumulates resentment fast. Naming the imbalance — out loud, without blame — prevents it from becoming something worse.
