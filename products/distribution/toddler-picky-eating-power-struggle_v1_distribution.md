**Title:** "The 'they'll eat when hungry' advice wasn't designed for my kid. Here's what actually worked."

**Body:**

My son (now 4.5) went through a period where he ate fewer than 10 foods. Not the normal toddler "ew broccoli" thing — a real, persistent, family-mealtimes-are-war situation. The "they'll eat when they're hungry" crowd meant well. But my kid actually would not eat when he was hungry, because he had narrowed his accepted foods down to the point where he was perpetually undershooting his caloric needs without realizing it.

I spent about six months working through this with a feeding therapist and a pediatrician. Here's what I learned that actually works, in order of impact:

**1. Remove pressure completely. Not mostly. Completely.**
Every negotiation, pleading, "just one bite," or "take a taste" is data for your kid's brain that the food is associated with adult conflict. The Division of Responsibility (parent controls what/when/where, child controls whether/how much) sounds too simple. It is that simple. It is also brutally hard to implement when you've spent years negotiating.

But it works. Pressure removal is the prerequisite for everything else.

**2. Count exposures, not rejections.**
Your kid rejected the peas for the 4th time? That's attempt 4 of 15. Research shows children need 10-15 exposures to a new food before they'll actually eat it. Most parents stop after 3. Keep going. The rejection is not data about whether they like it — it's data that more exposures are needed.

**3. The grazing problem.**
If your kid snacks throughout the day, they are never actually hungry at mealtimes. And if they're never hungry at mealtimes, the Division has nothing to work with. Structured meals — no food between dinner and breakfast, no snacking an hour before a meal — is the unglamorous backbone of this whole process.

**4. The biggest sign you need professional help.**
If your child is losing weight or falling off their growth curve, see a pediatrician before trying home strategies. Not instead of — alongside. A feeding evaluation (usually with an occupational therapist) is not scary or invasive. It's play-based and low-pressure. Most kids find it easier than home mealtimes.

I put together a free checklist that helps you figure out whether what you're dealing with is typical picky eating or something that needs professional input — and tracks your exposures so you know when to keep going.
