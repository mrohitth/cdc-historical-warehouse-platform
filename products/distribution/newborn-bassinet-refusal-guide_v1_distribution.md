## Reddit Bridge Post (r/Parenting)

**Title:** The transfer problem is not a sleep training problem — it's a neuroscience problem (and why "drowsy but awake" doesn't work for newborns)

---

Posted in r/Parenting:

---

I spent the first 6 weeks of my daughter's life as a human bassinet. I could not put her down. Not for a nap, not for a shower, not for a meal. The second her back touched the mattress she was awake and screaming.

I tried everything friends told me: "drowsy but awake," "let them cry it out a little," "they'll figure it out." None of it worked, and honestly, it made me feel like I was failing at something that should be simple.

What I eventually learned: the problem isn't that my baby "hadn't figured out" how to sleep. The problem was that her nervous system was doing exactly what it was designed to do.

**The Moro reflex is the problem no one talks about**

Newborns have a startle reflex — when their position changes suddenly, when they feel like they're falling, or when their head support shifts, their arms fly out and they cry. It's automatic and it's strongest in the first 3-4 months.

When you pick your baby up from the bassinet and then try to put them back, their body interprets the descent as falling. The reflex fires. They wake up.

This isn't stubbornness. It's neurology.

**Why "drowsy but awake" doesn't work for newborns**

"Drowsy but awake" is good advice for infants around 4 months and older who have the neurological development to self-settle. Newborns don't. A 3-week-old who is "drowsy but awake" is in light sleep — and light sleep is when they're most likely to startle awake at any environmental change.

The goal first has to be getting them into deep sleep before you attempt anything.

**What actually works: the 5S foundation + slow transfer**

The method that finally worked for us was combining Dr. Harvey Karp's 5S technique (swaddle, side/stomach, shush, swing, suck) with a specific transfer protocol:

1. Get the baby into deep sleep first — not drowsy, actually asleep, limp and unresponsive to mild stimulation
2. Lower them feet-first (maintains head elevation, reduces falling sensation)
3. Keep one hand on their chest with steady pressure after placement
4. Shush continuously for 60 seconds after
5. If they startle, use shush and pat for 60 seconds before picking up — most will settle without being lifted

The key insight: test for deep sleep readiness by gently lifting their arm an inch and releasing it. No reaction = ready to transfer.

This won't work every time — nothing does — but it gave me my arms back.

**The longer view**

The transfer problem peaks in the first 3-4 months and resolves as the Moro reflex naturally weakens. Most babies who are worked with a consistent method are transferring successfully by 8-12 weeks. By 4 months, many are sleeping independently. It's a phase.

If you're in it right now: you're not failing. Your baby is doing exactly what their nervous system is wired to do. You just need the right bridge.
