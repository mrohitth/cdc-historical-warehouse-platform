**Title:** "Got a $2,800 garage door quote. Here's what I learned about not getting taken."

**Body:**

A garage door spring snapped last month. I got three quotes: $2,800, $1,100, and $950. Same repair.

I spent a few hours learning how garage door pricing actually works. Here's what's worth knowing before you sign anything:

**1. The most overdiagnosed part is the sensor.**
Before you call anyone for a door that closes and immediately re-opens: look at the two small sensors at the base of the door frame. Loosen the mounting bracket, adjust until both indicator lights turn solid green, retighten. This fixes it roughly 50% of the time. Cost if it works: $0.

**2. Get three written bids, and ask for parts vs. labor separately.**
When I asked the $2,800 contractor what the parts cost, he said "I don't break it down that way." That was the moment I knew.

**3. The spring replacement average is about $950 per spring — and springs always fail in pairs.**
Contractors know this. If one spring broke, they should quote replacing both (since the other is close behind). But the price per spring on the quote should reflect actual part costs ($80-$250 for torsion springs, not $600).

**4. Buy your own opener if the motor is the problem.**
A compatible opener from Home Depot costs $150-$400. Labor to install is $75-$125. A contractor's bundled opener price typically runs $500-$900. If the opener is the issue, supply it yourself and pay labor only.

**5. The Angi's/HomeAdvisor problem.**
These platforms charge contractors 20-40% of the job to get the lead. That commission gets baked into your quote. Find a local company with a Google listing instead — no platform tax.

I put together a checklist with all the questions to ask on every quote — it covers sensors, springs, openers, and the repair-vs-replace decision. It's free.
