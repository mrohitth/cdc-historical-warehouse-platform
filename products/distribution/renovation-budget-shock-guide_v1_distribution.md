**Post Title (ask):** What nobody told you before your first renovation: the budget system I wish I'd had

**Post Body:**

First renovation. I thought I knew what I was getting into.

I'd read the cost guides, watched the YouTube videos, talked to friends who'd done it. I had a number in my head — $35,000 for a kitchen update — and I stuck to it.

Eight weeks in, I'd spent $47,000 and the project wasn't finished.

What I didn't understand: the budget number you start with isn't the budget you end with. It's the starting point of a process that involves contingencies you can't predict, scope decisions you haven't made yet, and change orders that feel optional in the moment and mandatory once the walls are open.

I spent the next year talking to contractors, other renovators, and a few who specialize in renovation finance. I wanted to understand what the people who *didn't* go over budget had in common.

Three things stood out:

**1. They built budgets from real local data, not national averages.**
National averages hide a 5x cost range. A kitchen remodel "averages" $25,000–$75,000. The person who anchors to $25,000 is the person who runs out of money at week three. The person who calls three cabinet suppliers and two flooring companies in their zip code before setting a budget — that's the person who knows what they're actually building.

**2. They used a contingency buffer as a planning tool, not emergency cash.**
The standard advice is 20% contingency for modern homes, 30% for older ones (pre-1980). Most people treat this as "extra money if things go wrong." The renovators who stayed on budget treated it as part of the budget — a fund that either gets spent on contingencies or gets reallocated to Tier 3 wish-list items once the project closes clean.

**3. They got three bids and compared them line by line.**
This isn't about finding the cheapest contractor. It's about understanding what the market looks like, what normal markup looks like, and what one contractor is including that another left out. If one bid is 40% lower than the others — that's not a reason to celebrate. That's a reason to ask questions.
