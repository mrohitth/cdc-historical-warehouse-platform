**Title:** The contact nap thing — a thread on what's actually happening and what you can do about it (no cry-it-out required)

**Body:**

My partner and I spent 9 weeks with a contact nap baby. Not by choice. By necessity. Our kid would wake up the second we transferred him to the bassinet. We tried everything short of sleep training, which we weren't ready for.

I want to share what's actually going on and what actually helped us — not as experts but as parents who read a lot, asked a lot of questions, and eventually found a path through.

---

**What's actually happening:**

Newborns have a few things working against them:

- **The startle reflex** is real. When your baby feels unsupported mid-air — like when you lift them out of the bassinet — their arms fly out and they wake up. This is a documented reflex that peaks around 2-3 months and fades by 4-6 months. It's not a sign your baby is "difficult." It's a developmental stage.

- **Temperature regulation** is immature. Your baby can't maintain body temperature the way you can. The contrast between your body heat and a cool crib mattress is enough to trigger a wake-up. This is especially true in winter or in rooms with AC.

- **Sleep cycles are short** — 30-45 minutes in the first months. Your baby spends more time in active (REM) sleep than adults do, and they can't transition between cycles without the same conditions that put them to sleep. That's not stubbornness. That's physiology.

**What we found helped:**

A gradual approach, not an extinction approach. Here's the quick version of what we did:

**Stage 1 — The 20-minute rule.** Before you move your baby, wait at least 20 minutes after they fall asleep. This is the minimum time for a newborn to reach deep sleep. Moving them during light sleep almost always triggers a wake-up.

**Stage 2 — The slow transfer.** When you do move them, move like you're underwater. The last 6 inches should take 10-15 seconds. Lower them slowly. Keep one hand on their chest for 30 seconds after they're down. If they stir, don't pick them up immediately — just hold still.

**Stage 3 — The drowsy put-down.** Once the first stage is working more often than not, try putting them down when they're drowsy but not fully asleep — eyes still mostly closed. This teaches them that falling asleep in the crib is possible, not just in your arms.

**Stage 4 — Gradual withdrawal.** Progressively increase distance from the crib over days — from touching them, to sitting beside the crib, to sitting a few feet away, to briefly leaving the room.

This process takes weeks, not days. It does not require your baby to cry it out. It does require patience and consistency.

**The survival part:**

Contact naps are a parent exhaustion problem as much as a baby sleep problem. If you're in it right now:

- Split shifts with your partner so each of you gets at least one 4-hour block of uninterrupted sleep per night
- Use a carrier if you need your hands free — just don't fall asleep with baby in the carrier on a soft surface
- If you're falling asleep with your baby in unsafe positions, please look up safe cosleeping basics (not an endorsement — just safety information). Exhaustion is dangerous.
